Family therapy is different from individual therapy in a fundamental way: the unit of focus is the family system rather than one person. That distinction shapes everything — who attends, what gets explored, and what change looks like.

Here is what family therapy actually involves and what to expect.

## Who is in the room

Family therapy typically includes the family members most involved in the patterns or conflicts being addressed. That does not always mean every person in the household. Depending on what is being worked on, a session might include parents and one or more children, just the two parents, a single parent and a teenager, or a mix that shifts over time.

The therapist decides with the family who to include at what point. There is no rule that every member must be present at every session — sometimes individual sessions or subgroup sessions are useful alongside full family sessions.

## What the therapist is looking for

A family therapist pays attention to patterns — not just what each person says, but how family members interact with one another. Who speaks for whom. Who goes quiet when the topic shifts. What happens in the room when conflict surfaces. How the family manages disagreement, emotion, and connection.

This perspective comes from systems theory: the idea that families develop organized patterns of relating, and that individual symptoms or problems often make more sense in the context of those patterns than in isolation. A teenager's withdrawal, a parent's chronic frustration, or a recurring argument are all pieces of information about how the system is working.

## What sessions look like

Family therapy sessions are more active and less linear than individual therapy. The therapist may guide conversation between family members rather than directing everything to themselves. They may interrupt patterns as they are happening in the room, offer observations, or ask each member a question and watch how others respond.

It can feel uncomfortable, particularly at the start — having difficult conversations in front of a therapist, with family members present, is different from talking about those things privately. That discomfort is often productive. The therapist's role is to create a structure where difficult things can be said and heard.

## What family therapy is good for

Family therapy is particularly useful when:

- **The problem involves the whole system** — a recurring conflict, communication that has broken down, or dynamics that every member is participating in
- **A child or teenager is struggling** — when an adolescent's difficulties are affecting the family, or when family dynamics are contributing to the individual's distress, addressing the system often works better than individual therapy alone
- **A transition has destabilized the household** — divorce, a new marriage, a blended family, a loss, a parent's illness
- **Communication has become painful or impossible** — when family members have stopped being able to have honest conversations, a therapist provides the structure to make it possible again

## What family therapy cannot do

Family therapy requires genuine participation from the people involved. If one member is entirely unwilling or unable to engage in the work, it limits what is possible. A therapist can work with that — sometimes one-on-one sessions run alongside family sessions, or the family configuration shifts — but the more honest participation there is, the more useful the work tends to be.

Family therapy also does not require everyone to agree on what the problem is. People can enter with very different views of what is going on. Navigating those differences is often the starting point.
