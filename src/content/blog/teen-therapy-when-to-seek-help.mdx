Adolescence is genuinely hard. Separating from parents, building an identity outside the family, navigating peer relationships, academic pressure, and the first real encounters with loss, rejection, and failure — these are real stressors, not minor background noise.

The question parents often struggle with is: how do I know when what my teenager is going through is within the range of normal adolescent difficulty, and when does it suggest they might benefit from professional support?

There isn't a bright line. But there are patterns worth paying attention to.

## Behavioral and emotional signals to notice

### Withdrawal that goes beyond needing space

Teenagers normally pull away from parents. That's healthy. What's different is withdrawal that crosses into isolation — pulling away from friends, dropping activities they used to care about, spending most of their time alone in their room, and not because they're recharging but because the outside world feels too difficult.

### Mood that is consistently low, irritable, or flat

A teenager who seems sad most of the time — not just sometimes — or who is chronically irritable in a way that goes beyond the expected friction of adolescence, may be experiencing depression or anxiety that has become persistent enough to interfere with their functioning.

Flat affect — the absence of emotional expression rather than overt sadness — can be easy to miss. It can look like a teenager who just doesn't seem to care about anything anymore, when something more is happening underneath.

### Changes in sleep, appetite, or academic functioning

Significant changes in sleep (sleeping far more or far less), appetite, or school performance can signal that something is off. A teenager who goes from functioning well academically to struggling, or who stops eating regularly, often isn't simply being lazy or difficult — there's usually something underneath it worth understanding.

### Expressions of hopelessness or statements about not wanting to be alive

Take these seriously, even if they seem casual or offhand. Not every expression of this kind is a plan, but all of them warrant a direct conversation. "I don't want to be here anymore" might be venting, or it might be telling you something important. You can ask directly: "When you say that, what do you mean?" A therapist can help assess the level of concern.

If you believe your teenager is in immediate danger, call 988 (the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline) or go to the nearest emergency room.

### Significant changes in peer relationships

Losing friendships, becoming involved with a peer group that concerns you, or abruptly cutting off existing relationships are all worth paying attention to — not as signs that require immediate intervention, but as data points that suggest something is shifting.

## When to consider therapy

There's no threshold your teenager has to reach before therapy is appropriate. Some teenagers benefit from working with a therapist during difficult transitions — a move, a parents' divorce, the loss of a friendship — without anything being "wrong" in a clinical sense. Therapy is a resource, not a last resort.

That said, the patterns above — persistent low mood, isolation, functional decline, expressions of hopelessness, or significant changes in behavior — are clearer signals that support beyond what a family can provide may be useful.

## How to bring it up with your teenager

This matters. Teenagers who feel forced into therapy rarely engage with it usefully. The conversation works better when it's honest and doesn't catastrophize: "I've noticed you seem like you've been having a harder time lately. I've been thinking it might be worth talking to someone — not because something is wrong with you, but because having a space outside the family sometimes helps."

You can also invite them into the selection process. Showing them a therapist's profile, letting them have some input into who they see, and acknowledging that they get to decide what to talk about in sessions all reduce the feeling of being managed rather than supported.
