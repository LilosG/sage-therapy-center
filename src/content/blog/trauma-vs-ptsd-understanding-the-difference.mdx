"Trauma" and "PTSD" are often used interchangeably in everyday conversation — and in much of the mental health content online. They are related, but they are not the same thing. Understanding the distinction matters when you are trying to figure out what kind of help might fit what you are experiencing.

## What trauma actually means

Trauma, in a clinical context, refers to an experience or series of experiences that overwhelm a person's capacity to cope at the time they occur. The event does not have to be catastrophic by any external measure. What determines whether something is traumatic is not the size of the event but how it registers in the person's nervous system.

This means that the same event can be traumatic for one person and not for another. It also means that experiences that do not look dramatic — chronic neglect, emotionally chaotic households, sustained humiliation, accumulated smaller losses — can be as traumatizing as single acute events like accidents or assault.

## What PTSD actually means

Post-traumatic stress disorder is a specific clinical diagnosis. To meet criteria for PTSD, someone must have experienced a traumatic event and then have developed a defined cluster of symptoms that persist for more than a month and cause significant functional impairment. Those symptom clusters include:

- **Re-experiencing** — intrusive memories, flashbacks, nightmares
- **Avoidance** — staying away from people, places, thoughts, or feelings connected to the event
- **Negative changes in cognition or mood** — persistent negative beliefs about oneself or the world, emotional numbing, diminished interest
- **Changes in arousal and reactivity** — hypervigilance, exaggerated startle response, difficulty sleeping or concentrating

Not everyone who experiences trauma develops PTSD. Many people experience significant post-traumatic symptoms — distress, avoidance, emotional dysregulation — without meeting the full criteria for the diagnosis. Those people still benefit from trauma-informed therapy even if they do not carry a PTSD diagnosis.

## The difference matters for treatment

Because PTSD has a specific symptom profile and research base, treatments have been developed and studied for it specifically. EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing) is among the best-supported — recognized by the World Health Organization, the American Psychological Association, and the Department of Veterans Affairs as an evidence-based treatment for PTSD.

EMDR also works for trauma that does not meet full PTSD criteria, anxiety connected to specific past experiences, phobias with a traceable origin, and grief that has become stuck. The common thread is that there is unprocessed material — a memory or cluster of memories stored in the nervous system in a way that continues to generate distress — that is driving current symptoms.

## When EMDR may not be the right starting point

EMDR requires a degree of stabilization before processing begins. Someone in acute crisis, actively struggling with suicidality, or dealing with significant dissociation may need other work first — building safety and coping resources — before the memory processing phase is appropriate.

A good therapist trained in EMDR will assess whether processing is indicated and at what point. The protocol begins with history, preparation, and stabilization — not immediately with the distressing material.

## Complex trauma and a different frame

Single-incident trauma — a car accident, an assault, a discrete loss — tends to be more straightforwardly addressed with EMDR. Complex trauma — trauma that occurred in childhood, in attachment relationships, or over extended periods — often requires a more layered treatment approach. This may still include EMDR at certain stages, but the work of building safety, processing, and integration takes longer and is more nuanced.

## What therapy for trauma looks like at S.A.G.E.

Kristin Moorehead-Malley, LMFT, is trained in EMDR and draws on it as part of an individualized approach to trauma-focused therapy at S.A.G.E. Therapy Center in Carlsbad. The approach takes into account whether single-incident or complex trauma is present, what stage the person is in, and what combination of methods — EMDR, psychodynamic work, attachment-based approaches, somatic awareness — is most likely to be useful.

A formal PTSD diagnosis is not required to begin. If past experiences are affecting how you feel, function, or relate to others, that is enough of a starting point to discuss with a therapist.
