Telehealth therapy — also called online therapy or video therapy — means meeting with a licensed therapist by secure video rather than in an office. In California, any licensed therapist can provide telehealth to clients anywhere in the state, which has made it a common option for people whose schedules, location, or preferences make in-person sessions difficult.

Here is what telehealth therapy actually involves, how it compares to in-person work, and what to expect if you are considering it.

## How telehealth sessions work in practice

A telehealth session typically uses a HIPAA-compliant video platform — not a standard consumer app like Zoom or FaceTime. Your therapist provides a link or portal login. You join at the scheduled time, usually from a private location: a home office, a parked car, a room with a closed door.

The session structure is the same as in-person therapy. You and your therapist talk. The work — exploring what brought you in, identifying patterns, building understanding — happens through the conversation, the same as it would across a desk.

## Is telehealth therapy as effective as in-person sessions?

The research supports telehealth as effective for most common presentations. Studies on video-delivered therapy for anxiety, depression, trauma, and couples work have found outcomes comparable to in-person sessions. EMDR has also been adapted for telehealth delivery with good results.

The evidence is not that telehealth is universally equivalent to in-person therapy for all people and all concerns. Certain presentations — significant dissociation, acute crisis, some complex trauma — may benefit from the additional containment of in-person work. But for most people seeking therapy for anxiety, relationship patterns, personal growth, life transitions, or depression, telehealth is a clinically sound option.

## What telehealth suits well

Telehealth works well when:

- **Commuting is difficult** — your schedule, distance, or caregiving responsibilities make driving to an office consistently hard
- **Privacy matters** — you prefer to have the conversation from your own space, without running into someone in a waiting room
- **You travel frequently** — telehealth keeps continuity when you are not in your home city
- **The work is verbal** — for therapy focused on talking through experience, patterns, and meaning, the format makes little clinical difference

## What may work better in person

In-person sessions may be worth considering when:

- You find it difficult to settle into video calls or feel the connection through a screen
- The work involves significant somatic or body-based processing that benefits from physical presence
- You value the ritual of leaving your environment for a dedicated space

Some people use both — in-person for certain phases of the work, telehealth when logistics require it.

## Finding a private space for telehealth sessions

The most common practical challenge with telehealth is finding privacy. A few options people use: a home office with a closed door, a car in a quiet parking area, a white noise machine outside the door to reduce sound transmission, or noise-canceling headphones.

It does not need to be perfect. It needs to be private enough that you can speak without concern.
