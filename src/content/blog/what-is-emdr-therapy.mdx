EMDR stands for Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing. It was developed in the late 1980s by psychologist Francine Shapiro and has since become one of the more researched treatments for trauma and PTSD. The American Psychological Association, the World Health Organization, and the Department of Veterans Affairs all recognize it as an evidence-based treatment for trauma.

Here's what it actually involves and what makes it different from more traditional talk-based therapy.

## The core idea behind EMDR

Most therapy for trauma involves talking about what happened — building a narrative, processing emotions, gradually reducing the charge of the memory over time. EMDR does something different.

The theory behind EMDR is that traumatic memories sometimes get "stuck" in an unprocessed state — stored in the nervous system in a way that retains the emotional intensity of the original experience. When something triggers a stuck memory, the emotional and physical response can feel as vivid as the original event, even if you're aware you're safe now.

EMDR works by activating the distressing memory while simultaneously engaging in bilateral stimulation — typically side-to-side eye movements, but sometimes alternating taps or sounds. The bilateral stimulation appears to facilitate the brain's natural processing of the memory, allowing it to be stored in a way that reduces its emotional charge.

You still know what happened. The factual memory doesn't disappear. What changes is the way the memory feels when it surfaces.

## What bilateral stimulation actually looks like

During EMDR processing, you hold a specific memory or distressing image in mind while following the therapist's finger, a light bar, or another visual cue moving back and forth. Sessions alternate between brief periods of bilateral stimulation and pauses where the therapist checks in about what's coming up.

The session doesn't involve hypnosis and you remain fully aware and in control of the process throughout. If a session stirs up more than feels manageable, the therapist adjusts the pace.

## The eight phases of EMDR

EMDR isn't a single technique — it's a structured eight-phase protocol:

1. **History and treatment planning** — identifying what to work on and what the current concerns are
2. **Preparation** — establishing safety, stabilization, and the bilateral stimulation method
3. **Assessment** — identifying the specific memory, image, belief, and physical sensation to target
4. **Desensitization** — the bilateral stimulation processing work
5. **Installation** — reinforcing a more adaptive belief to replace the negative one
6. **Body scan** — checking for remaining physical tension or distress
7. **Closure** — returning to a stable state at the end of each session
8. **Reevaluation** — checking progress at the start of the next session

Not every session moves through all eight phases. Early sessions may focus on history and preparation. Later sessions handle the actual processing.

## What EMDR is commonly used for

EMDR was developed for PTSD and trauma, and that remains its most documented application. Research supports its use for:

- **Post-traumatic stress disorder** — including single-incident trauma and complex, repeated trauma
- **Anxiety** — particularly when anxiety is linked to specific memories or experiences
- **Phobias** — especially those with a traceable origin in past experience
- **Grief** — particularly complicated or traumatic loss
- **Performance anxiety** — sometimes relevant in sports psychology or performance contexts

It is not a universal treatment for every mental health concern and isn't the right fit for everyone. Therapists who are trained in EMDR can assess whether it's likely to be useful for a specific person's situation.

## How EMDR is different from talk therapy

In traditional talk therapy, a lot of the work happens through language — putting experience into words, exploring meaning, building understanding. EMDR can move through material that's difficult to articulate verbally, because the processing happens through the bilateral stimulation rather than primarily through verbal description.

This makes it useful for trauma experiences that feel too overwhelming to discuss directly, or for people who feel they've already talked extensively about something without the emotional charge reducing.

## What to expect if you're considering EMDR

EMDR sessions are typically 50–90 minutes. Processing sessions can be emotionally demanding, and it's common to feel tired afterward. Distress sometimes surfaces between sessions as processing continues — this is expected and normal, and a good therapist will prepare you for it and provide tools to manage it.

Most people don't see change from a single session. The number of sessions needed varies widely depending on the complexity of what's being addressed — single-incident trauma tends to resolve more quickly than complex, relational, or developmental trauma.
